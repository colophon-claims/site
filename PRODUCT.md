# Product

## Register

brand

## Platform

web

## Users

People who have a benchmark and a number, and now have to get someone to believe it. Benchmark authors publishing their own results. Teams that just got a score and know it will be argued with. Auditors and maintainers who want a number they can stand behind. They arrive skeptical, often mid-argument, with no time to read a manifesto. The job: make my number something I can point at instead of something I have to defend.

## Product Purpose

Colophon turns a benchmark claim from "trust me" into "check me." The method is locked before the run, timestamped by someone who isn't you. Every result is counted, failures included. The whole thing can be recomputed by any stranger with one command, free, forever. When a number is doubted, the reader doesn't argue; they hand over the seal.

Two ways in: seal your own run, or have Colophon run it so "who ran it" is answered by someone independent. Checking is free and stays free. Nothing on the site is for sale; the closing section invites a conversation only.

Success: a visitor with a benchmark understands in ten seconds that they can seal a claim here and walk away with something a stranger can check, and then does it.

## Brand Personality

Exact, plain, calm.

The reader arrives expecting to be sold to, and a brand that raises its voice confirms the suspicion. This one doesn't need to; it can point. Exact: precision is the personality, not a feature. Plain: it says what it proves and what it doesn't, in short sentences, with no adjectives doing the work. Calm: the confidence of someone holding the evidence; nothing urgent, nothing flashing. The emotional goal for the reader is relief: "I don't have to defend this any more."

References, with the specific thing that fits: the browser padlock (small, unglamorous, universally read; nobody thinks about the cryptography behind it; the seal should be designed to be read like that, a mark you recognise rather than a badge that boasts); arXiv (trusted precisely because it does not try to convince you; permanent addresses, no marketing, the content is the credibility; reports and their evidence should feel that permanent); a notarised document (a stamp, a date, a signature; evidence you would hand across a table).

## Anti-references

The AI-startup landing page: gradient hero, a "trusted by 10,000 teams" logo strip, three feature cards with icons. That is the vendor-marketing aesthetic the reader has learned to dismiss.

Leaderboard and analytics dashboards. Ranking is the thing this product refuses to do; it must not look like one.

Anything that reads as a pitch. The closing section invites a conversation; it never closes a sale.

## Design Principles

- The reader's problem first. Every section starts from "my number is about to be doubted," not from the machinery.
- Practice what you preach. Every number on the site is itself checkable; the site never asks for trust it doesn't earn on the page.
- Honest about limits. What a seal proves and what it doesn't (a run you did yourself versus one someone independent did) is said plainly, because the honesty is the credibility.
- Show the seal, don't describe it. A real sealed claim on the page beats a paragraph about sealing.
- Nothing promised that doesn't exist.
- No insider vocabulary. If a word needs the system's manual to be understood, it does not appear on the page. Say what it does in the reader's words, or show it. Very, very clear.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

WCAG AA throughout. The seal's state is never carried by colour alone. Reduced motion is respected, and there should be almost none. Fully keyboard-navigable. The site makes no external requests: no analytics, no cookies, no CDN scripts, self-hosted fonts. A trust product that phones home undercuts itself.
